HIPAA for Dental Offices: One Patient Complaint Just Cost a Solo Practice $140,000

A Pennsylvania dental practice paid $140,000 to settle HIPAA violations found after one patient's records complaint, and the MCNA breach of 8.9 million dental records heads to its final settlement hearing on November 16, 2026. What OCR asks for first, and the eight basics a dental office needs in place before an investigator asks.

No hacker. No ransomware. No headline breach.

On October 8, 2026, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services announced that Shen Smiles, a small dental practice in Pennsylvania, agreed to pay $140,000 to settle HIPAA Privacy Rule violations. The whole case started with one former patient who asked for her records, more than once, and never got them.

If you run a dental office and your HIPAA program is a binder nobody has opened since it was printed, this is the case worth reading. Because what OCR found underneath that one complaint is the part that applies to almost every small practice.

What Actually Happened at Shen Smiles

The timeline matters, because none of it was fast:

  • A patient's attorney complained to OCR in April 2020 after repeated records requests went unanswered.
  • The practice told OCR the records could not be provided because a workforce member had stolen them.
  • OCR asked for the practice's HIPAA policies and procedures. For request after request, the answer came back: they did not exist.
  • What investigators found instead, per OCR's Notice of Proposed Determination: paper patient records stored in unsecured clear plastic boxes, some on the floor, some in a private bathroom. Records carried back and forth between office locations and the dentist's home. Records that often could not be located when the patient was sitting in the office. Staff who had never received formal HIPAA training. And the employee accused of taking the records had not been disciplined, with no step taken to stop it happening again.
  • OCR proposed a civil money penalty in July 2024. The practice asked for a hearing. Before the hearing happened, it settled in August 2026 and agreed to pay $140,000.

Six years from complaint to settlement. That is the part of HIPAA enforcement nobody prices in: even when you settle, you live with the case for years.

The Number That Should Get Your Attention

OCR calculated the maximum penalty at $1,400,000: $700,000 for each of two violations (no policies and procedures, and failure to reasonably safeguard patient information). Then OCR cut it by 90 percent for one reason: the practice is small and rural, and the full amount might have put it out of business.

Small size reduced the check. It did not reduce the violation count, and it did not make the case go away. The practice still paid $140,000, still spent years in the process, and is now the named example in OCR's 56th Right of Access enforcement action.

So the answer to "we are too small for OCR to care" is on the record now. OCR cared about a solo practice in rural Pennsylvania because one patient complained. A complaint is all it takes to open the file. What OCR finds after the file is open is up to you.

The Other End of the Scale: MCNA and the 8.9 Million Record Breach

Shen Smiles is what enforcement looks like with no breach at all. MCNA is the other end.

Managed Care of North America administers dental benefits, including Medicaid and CHIP dental programs. In early 2023, an unauthorized third party got into its systems and potentially viewed or took information on 8,923,662 people: names, addresses, birth dates, Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers, insurance and Medicare/Medicaid IDs, and details of dental and orthodontic care.

Three and a half years later, that breach is still not finished. Twenty-five lawsuits were consolidated into one case. The settlement, which MCNA entered while denying wrongdoing, covers up to $6.4 million in attorneys' fees, up to $2,500 per person for documented losses, and two years of medical data monitoring for the class. The claim deadline passed on October 19, 2026. On November 16, 2026, a federal judge in Florida holds the final approval hearing that decides whether the settlement stands.

Put the two cases side by side and the pattern is the real lesson:

  • Shen Smiles: no breach, one complaint, $140,000, because the basic program did not exist on paper or in practice.
  • MCNA: one breach at a benefits administrator, 8.9 million people, years of litigation, millions in costs, still not closed.

Different sizes, same root: patient information was not being protected the way the rules require, and the bill arrived later, with interest, in the form of legal time.

What OCR Actually Asks For When the Letter Arrives

Notice what OCR requested from Shen Smiles first. Not firewall logs. Not a penetration test. Documentation:

  • Your policies and procedures for patient access to records
  • Your breach notification policies and procedures
  • Evidence that PHI is safeguarded, on paper and on screen
  • Evidence of staff training

"None" is the most expensive answer in HIPAA. If a document does not exist, you cannot produce it, and an investigation that started with one narrow complaint widens into everything the missing documents were supposed to cover.

The practical version for a dental office, the things that need to exist before anyone asks:

1. A written right-of-access procedure with a clock on it. Patients get their records within 30 days, with one 30-day extension allowed if you notify them properly. Somebody in the office owns that clock. Requests get logged when they arrive, not remembered later. Most practices can produce records from Dentrix or Open Dental in minutes. The failure mode is not the software, it is a request sitting in an inbox or a paper folder with no owner.

2. A current risk analysis. The Security Rule has required one all along: an accurate, thorough assessment of the risks to electronic PHI, kept current as your systems change. The proposed Security Rule update you may have read about is still a proposal, with final action projected for July 2027. That is a planning date, not a deadline, and it changes nothing about what is enforced today.

Our full guide to the HIPAA IT requirements for dental offices covers the technical safeguards behind that risk analysis in detail.

3. Access that ends when employment does. The Shen Smiles records were, by the practice's own account, taken by a workforce member, and nothing changed afterward. In a modern office the equivalent is shared logins, a former employee whose Dentrix and remote access still work, and no list of who can see what. Unique user IDs, prompt offboarding, and an access review a few times a year close most of this.

4. Safeguards for paper, not just servers. Locked storage for charts. Nothing patient-related going home in a box. If your practice is paperless, the same thinking applies to the scanner output folder and the front desk printer tray.

5. Training you can prove. Annual HIPAA training for every workforce member, documented with names and dates. Undocumented training reads to an investigator as no training.

6. Records you can actually find. A records request you cannot fulfill because the chart cannot be located is a safeguard failure, not bad luck. For electronic records, this is where backups and restore tests stop being an IT topic: a backup that has never been test-restored does not prove you can produce anything.

7. A business associate agreement with every vendor that touches PHI. Your practice management software, your imaging platform, your patient communication tool, your IT provider, and the newer one practices are adding fast: AI tools that answer phones, write notes, or read charts. If a tool receives patient information and the vendor will not sign a BAA, that tool does not get patient information. No BAA, no PHI. That rule is simple, and it is the one most often skipped with new software because the contract step slows down the fun part.

8. A breach plan written before you need it. Who calls whom, who talks to patients, what the notification deadlines are. The Breach Notification Rule has its own timelines, and the week of an incident is the wrong week to learn them.

If you want these basics as a working list rather than a post, our HIPAA IT checklist is the same ground in check-off form.

None of this is exotic. That is the point. OCR's dental cases keep turning on the basics: policies that exist, training that happened, records that are secured and findable, access that is controlled. The practices that get hurt are the ones where those basics were assumed rather than done.

If Your Honest Answer Is "We Would Struggle to Produce That"

Then you are in the same position Shen Smiles was in, minus the complaint. The fix is unglamorous and mostly one-time work:

  1. Get the risk analysis done and written down.
  2. Write the policies, sized to a dental office, not copied from a hospital template nobody will follow.
  3. Train the team and keep the record.
  4. Clean up access: unique logins, offboarding checklist, vendor BAAs on file.
  5. Test that you can produce a complete patient record, chart, images, and documents, inside the 30-day window, on a normal busy day.
